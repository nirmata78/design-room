Clarifying question 1 of 6
Who subscribes, and what do they do with an event?
Merchant tills and accounting systems, through self-service registration; the partner bank, by contract.
Clarifying question 2 of 6
Must events for one loan arrive in order?
Not guaranteed across events. Merchants may fetch the loan's current state.
Clarifying question 3 of 6
How fast, and for how long?
First attempt within 5 s at p99 for a healthy endpoint. Retry for 72 hours.
Clarifying question 4 of 6
Is delivery exactly once?
At least once. Say so in the contract.
Clarifying question 5 of 6
Payload size, and one region?
About 2 KB. One region first.
Clarifying question 6 of 6
Who owns the event schema?
Producers own their events. Merchants must not break when producers change.

Theory · 1 of 6 · what it is
A platform that turns committed state changes into signed HTTP calls to endpoints we do not control, retries them for days, and never lets one endpoint take capacity from another.
Theory · 1 of 6 · what it is not

Four things it is confused with

Not a message broker

A broker lets consumers pull at their pace (SD-07). Here we push into servers that set no pace and give no back-pressure.

Not push notifications

Pushing to our own app (SD-12) goes to a platform we trust. Here every receiver is a stranger's server.

Not webhooks we receive

Receiving (SD-45) is about verifying others. Sending is about isolating others from each other.

Not exactly-once

Across a network into a system we do not control, the best honest promise is at-least-once plus an ID.

Theory · 2 of 6 · the properties

State safety and liveness separately

Safety · must never happen

S1 An event lost after its commit, or sent for a change that rolled back.
S2 A call to a private or internal address.
S3 An unsigned call, or one a third party could replay.

Liveness · must eventually happen

L1 Every event is delivered, or visibly dead, within 72 hours.
L2 A healthy endpoint is served within its p99 while others fail.
L3 A recovered endpoint drains its backlog without falling over again.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 1 of 6

M1 · A transactional outbox

Property it protects

S1.

How it works

The event is a row written in the same transaction as the change. A relay publishes unsent rows and marks them sent after the log acknowledges.

Failure it rules out

A lost event after a crash; an event for a rolled-back change.

What it costs

A table and a relay per producer; at-least-once into the log.

WITH due AS (
  SELECT d.id FROM delivery d JOIN endpoint e
    ON e.id = d.endpoint_id
   WHERE d.state = 'PENDING'
     AND d.next_attempt_at <= now()
     AND e.in_flight < e.cap
   LIMIT 200 FOR UPDATE OF d SKIP LOCKED)
UPDATE delivery SET state = 'IN_FLIGHT',
       lease_until = now() + '30 s'
  FROM due WHERE delivery.id = due.id
RETURNING delivery.id;

How it is builtEach endpoint is pinned to a dated public version; fan-out renders the internal event into it from a registry of every supported version. A producer's build fails if a supported version stops rendering.

Buy against build

At 180 M a month a managed delivery engine is ≈ $18k a month: a serious option if the bank allows it. Build past ≈ 800 M, or when data must stay.

On-call

The platform pages on its own lag and on success to healthy endpoints; merchants' failures go to them by email, never to our pager.

Top risks

A producer bypassing the outbox. A cap tuned too high. The proxy as a single point. A public version nobody owns.

What costs points in this round

Design

A POST inside the business request. One shared queue and pool. Retries in memory. Kafka retry topics as the per-endpoint queue.

Judgement

Promising exactly-once or strict order. Trusting a URL string. An API key instead of a signature. The internal event as the public contract. Paging on merchants' errors.

The close
We promise at-least-once and give every endpoint its own failure budget. Exactly-once is the consumer's job, and the contract that makes it possible is the product.
