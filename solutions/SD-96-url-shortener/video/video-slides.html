Requirements · functional

Four things the system must do

FR1 · Create

Long URL in, short link out. Optional alias and expiry. A retried request gets the same answer.

FR2 · Redirect

A request for a code gets the long URL as a redirect, or a not-found or gone page.

FR3 · Count

Clicks per link per day per country, visible to the owner within minutes.

FR4 · Withdraw

The owner deletes, or trust and safety blocks, a link. It stops redirecting everywhere within five minutes.

Requirements · scope

In, and out on purpose

In scope

Create, redirect, custom aliases, expiry, counts by day and country, takedown, a bulk API with a rate limit per key.

Out, on purpose

The malware classifier itself (a service we call). Accounts and billing. QR codes (a picture of the same link). Link-in-bio pages. Editing a link's target.

Editing a target is a takedown plus a create, as far as every cache is concerned.
The core
The core is three things: a code generator that never collides, a redirect path that is one memory read, and a side path for counting and withdrawal that never sits in front of it.

Chapter 3 of 9
Theory
What makes this problem hard, in words that outlive any product name
RequirementsEstimatesTheoryAPI + dataDesignChallengesCost · AUXRecap
Theory · 1 of 6 · what it is
A map from a short, unique, hard-to-guess code to a long URL, answered in milliseconds from anywhere, that can be withdrawn everywhere within a stated time.
Theory · 1 of 6 · what it is not

Four things it is confused with

Not a hash function

Hashing the URL is one way to make a key. It is not the system, and it is not unique once truncated.

Not a cache

The map is the source of truth. A cache is a copy that may be wrong for a bounded time.

Not a secret

Seven characters can be sampled. A link that must stay private needs authentication.

Not a CDN

The edge is a layer that answers for us, and that we must be able to take back.

Theory · 2 of 6 · the two properties

State safety and liveness separately

Safety · must never happen

S1 Two different long URLs are given the same code.
S2 A withdrawn code is answered by any layer after the takedown bound.

Liveness · must eventually happen

L1 A create gets a code without waiting for another create.
L2 A redirect for a live code answers within the target, even at a viral peak or with the store down.
L3 Every click is eventually counted, with a stated, measured loss.

Theory · 3 of 6 · the central tension

Speed of the answer against control over it

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 1 of 6

Counter ranges plus base-62

Property it protects

S1 and L1.

How it works

Each create server leases a block of 1,000 IDs from an allocator in one conditional write, then hands them out locally and encodes each in base-62.

Failure it rules out

Two servers handing out the same code; a lock or a round trip on every create.

What it costs

Gaps when a server dies holding a range: harmless at 290× headroom. Codes are sequential, unless mechanism 2.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 2 of 6

A keyed permutation

Property it protects

Non-enumerability.

How it works

A small Feistel network over the code space with a secret round key, plus cycle walking to stay below 62⁷. A bijection: distinct in, distinct out.

Failure it rules out

Scrapers walking codes in order; codes that reveal how many links we create.

What it costs

A key to keep and rotate. It is not secrecy: private links still need authentication.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 3 of 6

The unique constraint as the final authority

Property it protects

S1 for aliases and random codes.

How it works

Insert with a unique key on the code. The one shard that owns the code accepts one insert and rejects the other.

Failure it rules out

Check-then-insert races between two creators.

What it costs

A conflict answer the API must surface (409); a retry loop if codes are random.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 4 of 6

Cache-aside with an in-process hot cache and single flight

Property it protects

L2.

How it works

Shared cache in front of the store; a small in-process cache on every redirect server; concurrent misses for one code wait on one lookup.

Failure it rules out

A miss storm on one key; every viral click paying a network hop.

What it costs

Bounded staleness per layer, which the takedown budget must absorb. Memory on every server.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 5 of 6

An asynchronous click log

Property it protects

L2 and L3.

How it works

The redirect answers first, then appends a click event to a local buffer; batches go to a log partitioned by code; an aggregator counts per minute.

Failure it rules out

A counter update on the hot path; a hot row per viral link.

What it costs

Counts are minutes late; a small, stated loss on a crash, or a durable buffer if clicks are billed.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 6 of 6

Status, invalidation and bounded lifetimes everywhere

Property it protects

S2.

How it works

A status in the store; an invalidation event to every cache; a purge at the edge; and a maximum lifetime at every layer, chosen so their sum fits the bound.

Failure it rules out

A blocked phishing link still answered by a cache or an edge.

What it costs

Short lifetimes cost hit rate. A purge path to the CDN. 302 instead of 301.

Theory · 5 of 6 · the options for codes

Six ways to make a code

Theory · 5 of 6 · the pick, and what it gives up

D, with B as the simpler equal

Why D

No retry loop, ever; six-character codes possible for a premium tier; creation stays fast when the store is slow; coordination once per thousand links.

What it gives up

An allocator service to run, and a permutation key to manage. B needs neither: the constraint does all the work.

Choose B if creates stay under a few thousand a second and seven characters are always fine.

7.1 · Challenge 1

Interviewer · challenge 1 of 8

Challenge 1 of 8 · two servers, one code

Who decides that a code is free?

Naive answer“Generate a random code, check whether it exists, and insert it if it doesn't.”
BreakCheck-then-insert in the create service: the check is stale the moment it returns.
Challenge 1 of 8 · the trace

Two customers, one alias, three milliseconds apart

10:00:00.001server A: SELECT code  =  'sale' → none
10:00:00.002server B: SELECT code  =  'sale' → none
10:00:00.004server A: INSERT sale → shop-a.example
10:00:00.006server B: INSERT sale → shop-b.example: overwrites A's row
next dayA's printed flyers open B's page
Same race, random codesTwo servers draw aZ3kQ9x. Rare at 0.34 %, but at 400 creates a second, rare happens every day.
Challenge 1 of 8 · the fix

Disjoint ranges for generated codes; the constraint for the rest

MechanismEach server leases 1,000 IDs in one conditional write and hands them out locally. Every insert carries UNIQUE(code): one alias race ends in one 201 and one 409.
PropertyS1 is decided by the one place that sees every write for a code: its shard. L1: a create waits only on its own insert.
Challenge 1 of 8 · how ranges work

Coordination once per thousand links

Challenge 1 of 8 · sequence

A lease, then an alias conflict

Challenge 1 of 8 · the lease, as code

One conditional write per thousand links

def lease(store, size=1000):
    while True:
        cur = store.get("next_id")
        if store.compare_and_set("next_id", cur, cur + size):
            return range(cur, cur + size)   # ours alone
        # lost the race to another server: read again
Why it worksCompare-and-set makes leases disjoint. Inside a lease no coordination is needed; across leases none is possible to get wrong.
Interviewer · the second-level push
“The allocator is down. Or a server crashes holding half a range.”
Pause and answer before you continue
Challenge 1 of 8 · the second fix, and the honest limit

Runway, a replicated allocator, and the right failure

Runway

Lease the next block at half-used: 500 to 1,500 IDs in hand. At the 400/s peak over ten servers that is 12–37 s; at the 40/s average, two to six minutes.

A replicated allocator

A small consensus-backed store: losing one node is not an outage. 0.4 leases a second is no load.

A crashed server

Its unused IDs are skipped: a gap, not a collision.

If the allocator is down for minutes, creates stop. Redirects never depended on it. That is the right way round.
If asked, I'd say
“Each create server leases a block of a thousand IDs, so coordination happens once per thousand links, not once per link. The unique constraint on the code is the final authority, so an alias race ends in one success and one conflict. If the allocator goes down, servers have their leased runway, and redirects never depended on it.”
7.2 · Challenge 2
Codes in order
Interviewer · challenge 2 of 8
“Your codes come from a counter. What can someone learn from that?”

Challenge 2 of 8 · codes in order

Base-62 of a counter is a counter

Naive answer“Base-62 makes them look random.”
BreakThe encoder after the range: neighbouring IDs give neighbouring codes.
Challenge 2 of 8 · the trace

Our volume, and someone's private document, for free

Mon 09:00a user creates a link: 0000g8Q
Mon 09:10the same user: 0000gBA → 2,000 links in 10 minutes: our creation rate
Mon 09:11a scraper requests 0000g8R, 0000g8S, 0000g8T …
Mon 09:12reads every link created that morning, including an unlisted document share
What leaksBusiness volume to a competitor, and links their owners believed nobody would find.
Challenge 2 of 8 · the fix

A keyed permutation between the counter and the code

MechanismID → a four-round Feistel network over 42 bits with a secret key, cycle-walked below 62⁷ → base-62. Plus a rate limit on 404s per client.
PropertyUniqueness comes from the counter, unpredictability from the key. The two are separated, so neither weakens the other.
Challenge 2 of 8 · what the permutation does

Unique in, unique out, neighbours unrelated

Challenge 2 of 8 · the permutation, as code

Four rounds, then walk back into range

N = 62 ** 7                       # 3.5 × 10¹² codes
def permute(i, key):
    while True:
        l, r = i >> 21, i & (2**21 - 1)  # 42 bits, two halves
        for k in range(4):
            l, r = r, l ^ (prf(key, k, r) & (2**21 - 1))
        i = (l << 21) | r
        if i < N: return base62(i, width=7)  # cycle walk
Why it worksEach round is invertible, so the whole is a bijection on 42 bits. Walking until the value falls below 62⁷ keeps it a bijection on the code space.
Interviewer · the second-level push
“The key leaks. Or a customer says short links to private files must not be guessable at all.”
Pause and answer before you continue
Challenge 2 of 8 · the second fix, and the honest limit

Rotate the key; and a short code is not a secret

A leaked key

New codes use a new key with a version marker, or a fresh slice of the ID space. Old codes stay valid: they are stored, never recomputed.

Private content

42 bits can be sampled by an attacker with many addresses. Private files need authentication, or a long random token, not a short link. Say so to product.

If asked, I'd say
“A counter gives uniqueness, but base-62 of a counter is still a counter: it leaks our volume and lets anyone walk the links. I pass the ID through a keyed permutation, which keeps it unique and makes neighbours unrelated. And I'm clear that a short code is not a secret: private links need authentication.”

Challenge 3 of 8 · one viral link

One key, one shard, one row

Naive answer“It's cached, so it's fine.”
BreakThe shared-cache shard that owns the key, then the store when that shard restarts.
Challenge 3 of 8 · the trace

A hot key, then a cold one

14:02code k7Pq2mZ goes from 3 clicks/s to 40,000/s
14:02its cache shard: p99 0.8 ms → 9 ms; every other code on that shard slows too

Label the numbersIllustrative. The shape is the point: one key concentrates load that a fleet would spread.
Challenge 3 of 8 · the fix

Answer from memory on every server; one miss per server

MechanismAn in-process cache on every redirect server (a few hundred thousand entries, 30–60 s). Single flight: concurrent misses for one code wait on one lookup. Negative caching for unknown codes.
PropertyL2: the hot key's load is spread across all servers' memory. The store sees at most one query per server per code, not one per click.
Challenge 3 of 8 · sequence

A thousand requests, one lookup

Challenge 3 of 8 · single flight, as code

The first caller looks up; the rest wait

inflight = {}                       # code → future
def resolve(code):
    hit = local.get(code)
    if hit: return hit
    leader = code not in inflight
    fut = inflight.setdefault(code, Future())
    if leader:
        fut.set(shared_cache.get(code) or store.get(code))
        local.put(code, fut.value, ttl=30); inflight.pop(code)
    return fut.wait()
Why it worksHowever many clicks arrive, each server sends at most one lookup per code per lifetime: load on the store is servers × hot codes, not clicks.
Interviewer · the second-level push
“Now the in-process lifetime means a takedown takes up to a minute longer. And a whole region's servers restart together.”
Pause and answer before you continue
Challenge 3 of 8 · the second fix, and the honest limit

Budget the lifetime; warm before serving

Takedown

The 60 s lifetime is a line in the takedown budget, and the invalidation channel drops entries early. Challenge five.

Restarts

Roll them. A starting server warms its cache from the last hour's top codes before it takes traffic.

The real answer for a global viral link

The edge, a minute from every user. Challenge four.

If asked, I'd say
“A viral link is a hot key, not a load problem. I answer it from memory on every redirect server and coalesce misses, so each server sends one query, whatever the click rate. The cost is a short staleness window, which I count in the takedown budget.”

301 or 302?
Interviewer · challenge 4 of 8
“Should the redirect be a 301 or a 302?”

Challenge 4 of 8 · 301 or 302

An answer we cannot see, and cannot take back

Naive answer“301. It's permanent, and it saves servers.”
BreakThe browser: with a 301 it answers by itself, forever, and never asks us again.
Challenge 4 of 8 · the trace

Yesterday's click was the last one we saw

Mon 10:00a user clicks k7Pq2mZ; we answer 301; the browser caches it
Tue 09:00the same user clicks again: answered by the browser; not counted
Tue 15:00trust and safety blocks k7Pq2mZ: it now leads to a phishing page
Tue 15:20that user's browser still goes straight to the phishing page
Two lossesThe owner loses the count. We lose the ability to protect the user.
Challenge 4 of 8 · the fix

302 by default; a short, purgeable edge cache

Mechanism302 with browser caching off; the edge may keep it 60 s and can be purged. 301 only as an owner's opt-in for permanent, uncounted links.
PropertyControl (counting, and S2) stays at layers we can reach. The edge gives most of 301's saving at a bounded cost in freshness.

Interviewer · the second-level push
“Finance says the edge bill for ten billion requests is too high. Or edge-log counts arrive twenty minutes late.”
Pause and answer before you continue
Challenge 4 of 8 · the second fix, and the honest limit

Price both sides; say where exact counts come from

The edge bill

Compare the per-request edge cost with the servers and cross-region traffic it saves. Turn edge caching on per link, by click rate: only hot links need it.

Late counts

Edge-log counts carry their lag, shown as asOf. When clicks are billed, the count comes from our servers or a signed edge function, not from logs.

If asked, I'd say
“301 against 302 is a product decision, not an optimisation. A 301 is answered by the browser forever, so we lose the click and the ability to take a link down. I default to 302, let the edge cache it for a minute, and offer 301 only to owners who want a permanent, uncounted link.”

Challenge 5 of 8 · the second fix, and the honest limit

Lifetimes are the backstop; a 301 cannot be recalled

A lost event

The lifetimes are the backstop by design: about two minutes. An hourly test takedown measures the real time at every layer (challenge eight).

A 301

Cannot be recalled from a browser. That is why it is opt-in, and why the safety check runs before a 301 link is issued.

If asked, I'd say
“Every copy of the answer is a liability. A takedown writes the status, deletes the shared-cache key directly, sends an invalidation and purges the edge, and the lifetime at every layer is chosen so that even with a lost message the worst case is inside five minutes. A 301 can't be recalled, which is one more reason it isn't the default.”

Nine ways this round goes wrong

Design

  • Hash and truncate, with no collision story
  • Six characters without the arithmetic
  • Check, then insert
  • A counter update on every click
  • Codes that are a counter in disguise

Judgment

  • 301 by default
  • Forgetting every cached copy must be withdrawable

The redirect is one memory read. Codes are unique by blocks and a constraint, unguessable by a key. Everything else lives beside the redirect, with a number on how late it may be.
