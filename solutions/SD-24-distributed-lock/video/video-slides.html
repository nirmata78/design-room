Internal teams. Background workers electing a leader, per-entity exclusion before a write, and some “run this job once” schedulers.

Depends on the team. For some it corrupts records. For some it wastes an hour of compute. Tell me how you would find out.

About 10,000 acquisition attempts a second, around 5,000 lock names, holds from 100 ms to 30 s, default lease 10 s.
Clarifying question 4 of 6

p99 acquisition under 20 ms. Survive the loss of a minority of nodes. One region, three zones. Multi-region is a later question.

Mostly SQL databases and an object store. A few call partner HTTP APIs. Callers are JVM services. Assume pauses up to 15 s in the tail: a fact, not a tuning problem.

Requirements · the split that runs through everything

Two kinds of lock

Efficiency lock

A double hold wastes work: a job runs twice, an email is sent twice.

Needs: usually avoids it. Occasional failure is acceptable.

Correctness lock

A double hold corrupts data: two writers, a lost update, a double charge.

Needs: a token checked at the resource. No exceptions.

Most lock requests are idempotency problems in disguise. I ask which kind first.
The core
The core is four things: a lease, a replicated record, a token, and a check at the resource.

What makes this problem hard, in words that outlive any product name
RequirementsEstimatesTheoryAPI + dataDesignChallengesCost · AUXRecap
Theory · 1 of 6 · what it is
A small, consensus-replicated store of named leases: one holder at a time, for bounded time, with a number that orders every grant.
Theory · 1 of 6 · what it is not

Three things it is confused with

Not a mutex

A mutex lives in shared memory and can stop the holder. A distributed lock cannot reach into a paused process.

Not a queue

No payload, no work ordering. “One at a time per entity, in order” wants a single writer per partition.

Not a transaction

It protects nothing by itself. The resource has to take part.

Theory · 2 of 6 · the two properties

State safety and liveness separately

Safety · must never happen

Two parties both act as the holder of a name, and both effects take hold at the resource.

Liveness · must eventually happen

A free name is granted. A dead holder's name frees within one lease. A waiter is served, or told no, within its wait budget.

Why separate themA lock service that never grants anything is perfectly safe. One that grants everything immediately is perfectly live. Every fix later says which one it serves.
Theory · 2 of 6 · honesty about impossibility

You cannot have both, unconditionally

The result

In an asynchronous network, with even one process allowed to fail, no protocol guarantees agreement and always finishes. This is the F-L-P result.

What real systems do

Add a timing assumption, such as a failure detector with a timeout. They keep safety unconditional and lose liveness while a partition lasts.

Our position: safety unconditional. The minority side of a partition gets “unavailable”, never a second grant.
Theory · 3 of 6 · the central tension

You cannot tell a dead holder from a slow one

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 1 of 7

A lease with a TTL

Property it protects

Liveness.

How it works

A grant is valid for a stated time. When it ends, the service may give the name to someone else without asking the holder.

Failure it rules out

A lock held forever by a dead holder, and manual cleanup.

What it costs

A slow holder can be expired while alive. A crash stalls its name for up to one lease.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 2 of 7

A consensus-replicated record

Property it protects

Safety of the grant record.

How it works

Every grant is a log entry committed by a majority of replicas. One leader per term. A stale leader cannot commit.

Failure it rules out

Split brain, and a failover that forgets a grant.

What it costs

A quorum round trip on every write. No majority, no service. A cluster to run.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 3 of 7

A fencing token, checked at the resource

Property it protects

Safety at the resource.

How it works

Each grant carries a number larger than every earlier one for that name. The resource remembers the highest it accepted and rejects anything smaller.

Failure it rules out

A paused holder's stale write landing after a newer holder's.

What it costs

The resource must check. One number per protected row. It orders writes: it does not make a multi-step read consistent by itself.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 4 of 7

Monotonic clocks, and a conservative client deadline

Property it protects

Safety of the lease arithmetic.

How it works

Durations are measured on a monotonic clock. The client measures from when it sent the request and gives up at 0.7 of the lease.

Failure it rules out

Wall-clock steps and skew that make the client believe it holds a lock it lost.

What it costs

The client gives up early: 30% of the lease is wasted. It still assumes bounded drift.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 5 of 7

Sessions

Property it protects

Liveness and throughput.

How it works

A client holds one session, kept alive by one heartbeat. Locks hang off the session. When it dies, all its locks are freed in one command.

Failure it rules out

Per-lock renewal storms, and many separate expiries for one dead client.

What it costs

A coarser failure unit: one missed heartbeat drops every lock that client holds.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 6 of 7

A deterministic replicated state machine

Property it protects

Safety across replicas.

How it works

Every replica applies the same entries in the same order. The time used is written into the entry by the leader. Expiry is a command, not a background sweep.

Failure it rules out

Replicas that disagree about who holds a lock, or a follower expiring a lease on its own clock.

What it costs

Expiry waits for the leader's timer. A new leader must re-derive pending expiries.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 7 of 7

Bounded wait with directed hand-off

Property it protects

Liveness under contention.

How it works

Waiters queue per name, with a depth cap and a wait budget. Release grants to the head waiter in the same step.

Failure it rules out

A thundering herd on release, an unbounded queue, and polling that burns consensus.

What it costs

Queue state in the replicated machine. First-come order adds tail latency.

Theory · 5 of 6 · the pick, and what it gives up

D, behind our own API

What the pick buys

Safety proven by someone else's consensus. Leases and a revision number built in. One place to enforce rules, behind our A-P-I and S-D-K, so we can swap the substrate later.

What it gives up

One cluster's write ceiling, so we shard. Fairness, priority and shared modes. Cross-region reach. And the operating burden of a consensus cluster.

Always ask first: does the resource support a conditional write? If it does, that is row F, and it is cheaper.
Theory · 5 of 6 · a probe you may get

“Why not SELECT FOR UPDATE on a Postgres row?”

No lease

A row lock lives as long as the connection. A paused client keeps it. A dead one drops it only when the database notices.

No token

A held lock hands out no number the resource can check later.

When it is right

The resource is in the same database, so lock and work commit together. Or load is under a few thousand a second.

API · a question to dismiss aloud

Reentrancy

The question

Can a holder acquire a lock it already holds?

My answer

Yes, as a hold count in the S-D-K, keyed by session. The server keeps one grant per name. Doing it server-side doubles writes for no gain.

Scope it to a session, not a process, or a reconnecting client deadlocks against its own stale grant.

Data · the state machine

Apply is deterministic

// runs on every replica, in log order
apply(Acquire a, index, timeFromEntry):
  cur = held[a.name]
  // same owner retrying: return the same grant
  if cur live and cur.owner == a.owner: return cur
  if cur live: enqueue(a); return queued
  // token is the log index: monotonic, free
  held[a.name] = {a.owner, token: index,
                  expires: timeFromEntry + a.ttl}
  return granted(index)

High-level design · version 2 of 3

V2Replace the box with a consensus group

5 replicas·majority commit·token = log index

V2 · how the group works, in one slide

Leader, term, majority

One leader per term

All writes go to the leader. A new election starts a higher term. A node with an old term is ignored.

Commit = majority

3 of 5 replicas hold the entry. Any later leader must have been among them, so it has the entry.

No majority, no service

With 2 of 5, nothing commits. Safety is kept. Availability is not.

V2 · the objection

Nobody is told when the lock is lost

The caller cannot tell

A holder that loses its lease keeps working. Nothing tells it to stop.

Renewal is hand-rolled

Every team writes its own heartbeat, clock arithmetic and retries, and gets it subtly wrong.

Half the correctness lives in the client library. So the library is part of the product.

Lease timing in eight lines

Challenge 1 of 7 · the paused holder

What saves you when the holder wakes up?

Naive answer“Make the lease longer, and check the lease is still valid just before every write.”
Why it failsBetween the check and the write, the lease can still expire. The check and the write are not atomic.

Challenge 1 of 7 · the honest limit

No. Fencing orders writes. It does not repair a stale read.

t = 10.4 s  B acquires: token 42. Resource has only seen 41
t = 10.45 s B reads the invoice
t = 10.5 s  A (stale, token 41) writes: not smaller than 41, accepted
t = 10.6 s  B writes on its old read: A's update is lost
Fix 1Make B's critical section one conditional write against the version it read. The token is the version.
Fix 2Or B claims its token at the resource first. Then any later write by 41 is rejected.
Challenge 1 of 7 · the second fix, as a sequence

Claim first, then read

If asked, I'd say
“A lease cannot guarantee exclusion at the resource, because the holder cannot know it still holds the lock when its write lands. The proxy

The margin cannot see a pause. The token can.

MechanismOnly the majority commits. A stale leader steps down on a higher term. A new leader treats every session as alive for one lease.

Challenge 4 of 7 · why it works

The honest CP position

Safety · kept

Two replicas cannot commit. So a name never has two holders because of a partition.

Liveness · given up, on the small side only

The minority gets “unavailable”. A failover takes about 2 s. Tokens stay monotonic, because they are log indexes.

There is no clever third option. Interviewers listen for candidates who try to invent one.

The limit

Coverage

Recap · common mistakes in this round

Ten ways this round goes wrong

Design

  • A cache key with an expiry presented as a correctness lock
  • “Tune the lease” as the answer to a pause
  • No token, or a token nobody checks
  • Timestamps compared across machines
  • Failing open without saying so

Judgment

  • A beautiful consensus group and no word about the resource
  • Never asking whether a lock is needed
  • Offering atomic multi-lock
  • Restoring an old snapshot
  • Hiding the token in a thread-local
The close
The lock service decides who should be working. The resource decides who is allowed to write. Everything I built is there so those two answers can disagree safely.
