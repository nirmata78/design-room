Clarifying question 1 of 6
How many cards, and how much traffic?
8 million debit cards. 1,500 authorisations a second at the evening peak; 4,000 on the busiest shopping day.
Clarifying question 2 of 6
What is the deadline?
The network times out after about 2 seconds and stands in. Your own target is up to you.
Clarifying question 3 of 6
Which messages arrive?
Authorisation, incremental authorisation, reversal, advice, refund. Clearing comes by daily file.
Clarifying question 4 of 6
What about fraud?
Losses about 8 basis points of volume. Chargebacks arrive 30 to 90 days later. Analysts change rules several times a day, without a release.
Clarifying question 5 of 6
Where is the money?
The balance lives in the core ledger. Clearing amounts can differ from the authorisation; some never clear.
Clarifying question 6 of 6
Do we see card numbers?
No. The processor keeps full card numbers in its vault and sends a token. Keep it that way.
Requirements · functional

Four things the system must do

FR1 · Authorise

Answer an authorisation or incremental authorisation, approve or decline, inside the deadline. An approval places a hold.

FR2 · Reverse and advise

Reversals release holds. Stand-in advices are recorded and held, even past the balance.

FR3 · Clear

Match each clearing record to its hold, post the real amount, release or adjust the hold. Expire holds that never clear.

FR4 · Change rules

Analysts publish fraud rules as versioned data: backtest, shadow, slice, enforce. No deploy.

Requirements · non-functional

Our own approvals never take an account below zero. Every decision reproducible for 7 years. Clearing posted by 09:00. Degraded decisions inside an hourly cap.
Requirements · scope

In, and out on purpose

In scope

The decision, holds, reversals and advices, rules and model scoring, online features, the fail policy, clearing match and posting, decision events.

Out, on purpose

Issuing cards. The network and the processor. Disputes and chargebacks (we only read their labels). Statements. Model training. Credit products. The 3-D Secure challenge (its result is an input).

The core
The core is three things: a decision path that always answers inside the deadline, a hold ledger where each message takes effect once, and a clearing path that turns promises into posted money.

What makes this problem hard, in words that outlive any product name

Theory · 1 of 6 · what it is
An issuer's authorisation system answers the network before its deadline, reserves the amount as a hold against the available balance, and later settles the real amount from the clearing file.
Theory · 1 of 6 · what it is not

Four things it is confused with

Not a payment

No money moves at authorisation. Clearing and settlement move it, days later.

Not a debit

A hold can grow, shrink, be reversed, clear for a different amount, or expire.

Not the fraud model

The model is one input, with a budget. The decision is ours, with or without it.

Not the core ledger

Holds live next to the decision. Posted money lives in the ledger. The two are reconciled.

Theory · 2 of 6 · the two properties

State safety and liveness separately

Safety · must never happen

S1 Our own approvals take an account's available balance below zero.
S2 A network message takes effect twice: two holds, two postings.
S3 A decision cannot be reproduced: its inputs and versions are lost.

Liveness · must eventually happen

L1 Every authorisation gets an answer inside the deadline: ours, degraded if need be, never silence.
L2 Every hold ends: cleared, reversed or expired.
L3 A rule change takes effect within minutes.

Theory · 3 of 6 · the central tension

Decision quality against the deadline

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 1 of 7

Holds as ledger entries, with expiry

Property it protects

S1 and L2.

How it works

An approval inserts a hold; available = posted − active holds. Incrementals raise it, reversals release it, clearing moves it to posted, a lifetime by merchant type ends it.

Failure it rules out

Debiting at the till; tip and currency differences; money stuck forever.

What it costs

A hold lifecycle and an expiry job; a “pending” amount the customer must understand.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 2 of 7

Idempotency on the network reference, in any order

Property it protects

S2.

How it works

A message table keyed by the network's reference. A retry returns the stored answer. A reversal that arrives first is kept as a tombstone for its original.

Failure it rules out

A retried request placing two holds; an early reversal lost.

What it costs

A message store kept for longer than the network's retry window: days, not minutes.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 3 of 7

A per-account atomic reserve

Property it protects

S1, and S2 with mechanism 2.

How it works

One conditional write in the partition that owns the account: check available, insert the hold, record the message, bump the version.

Failure it rules out

Two concurrent swipes both seeing the same balance.

What it costs

A store partitioned by account; a shared business account becomes a hot key.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 4 of 7

A latency budget with a fail-policy table

Property it protects

L1.

How it works

Each step gets a slice; rules run in process; the model has a 60 ms cut-off; a table says what to decide when a step is late, with an hourly exposure cap.

Failure it rules out

A slow dependency making us miss the deadline.

What it costs

Degraded decisions with real exposure; a policy that risk and finance must own.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 5 of 7

Two-speed features

Property it protects

Decision quality, and S3.

How it works

Burst counters read and incremented inline, in one round trip; richer aggregates from the stream, seconds late; every feature value logged with the decision.

Failure it rules out

A stream that lags behind a burst; training on values the model never saw.

What it costs

A counter store on the hot path, and a second pipeline.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 6 of 7

Rules as versioned data

Property it protects

L3 and S3.

How it works

A sandboxed expression language. Backtest, shadow, slice, enforce. A kill switch, an expiry, and the rule-set version recorded on every decision.

Failure it rules out

A deploy to block an attack; one bad rule declining everyone.

What it costs

Tooling. And labels arrive in 30 to 90 days, so shadow cannot measure false positives.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 7 of 7

The clearing file as financial truth

Property it protects

L2, and books that agree.

How it works

Match by reference, then fuzzy. Move the amount from hold to posted. Post what has no match. Record stand-in decisions even when they overdraw.

Failure it rules out

Holds that never end; refusing advices or late clearing.

What it costs

An unmatched queue both ways; over-limit handling and collections.

Theory · 5 of 6 · the pick, and what it gives up

D, with E as the honest launch answer

Why D

The ledger is off the hot path. Holds and messages are reserved in one write. Rules change as data. The model can be late without making us late.

What it gives up

A second copy of balances, which must be reconciled with the ledger every day. E needs none of this, and fits a launch.

Pick E for the first months of a new card programme; move to D when fraud needs its own model.

API · two fields that matter

Merchant category and entry mode

Merchant category code

Restaurant, fuel, hotel, electronics. It sets the hold's lifetime, and it is the strongest single fraud feature.

Entry mode

Card present (chip, contactless) or not (online, phone). Most fraud is card-not-present, so almost every rule branches on it.

The decline reason matters too: “suspected fraud” sends the customer a text, and their “it was me” reply is the fastest false-positive signal we have.

Interviewer · the second-level push
“The clearing record has no matching authorisation. Or it arrives after the hold expired.”
Pause and answer before you continue
Challenge 1 of 8 · the second fix

The file is the financial truth

Reference, then fuzzy

No reference? Match card, merchant, amount within ±20 %, within 7 days.

No match: post anyway

The network has already paid the merchant from our settlement account. Post, and queue it for review.

After expiry: post anyway

It may take the account below zero: a modelled state, challenge 7. Expiry waits for the day's file.

Trap: one authorisation, one clearing record. Split shipments clear twice; some never clear; offline purchases clear with no authorisation.
If asked, I'd say
“Approval is a hold, not a debit. Available is posted minus holds, and clearing moves the real amount from hold to posted, matched by the network's reference. Holds that never clear expire; late clearing still posts.”

Who does thisA joint account. A phone wallet and a physical card. A subscription renewing while the customer pays at a till. Rare per account; daily across eight million.
Challenge 2 of 8 · the fix

One conditional write in the account's partition

MechanismIf holds + amount fit within posted and the version is unchanged: insert the hold, record the message, add to holds total, bump the version. Else re-read and decide again.
PropertyS1 is decided in the one partition that owns the account. Per-account traffic is a few a minute, so a conflict is rare and cheap.
Challenge 2 of 8 · sequence

One wins; the other re-reads and declines

Challenge 2 of 8 · the reserve, as code

The check and the write are one operation

def reserve(store, acct, ref, amount):
    while True:
        a = store.get("account", acct)
        if a.holds + amount > a.posted:
            return "decline"
        if store.transact(acct, [cond(version=a.version),
                put_hold(ref, amount), put_message(ref),
                set(holds=a.holds + amount, version=a.version + 1)]):
            return "approve"   # else: lost the race, read again
Why not a lockA lock gives the same safety with a lock service on the hot path and a lease to expire. The conditional write is the lock and the write in one step, in the store that owns the data.

Challenge 3 of 8 · the fix

Keyed by the network's reference, written with the hold

MechanismThe message table is keyed by the network reference. A retry gets the stored answer. A reversal with no original is kept as a tombstone. The record is written in the same transaction as the hold.
PropertyS2: the effect is keyed by what the network calls the message, not by when it arrives. No crash can separate a hold from its record.
Challenge 3 of 8 · sequence

A reversal that arrives first

Challenge 3 of 8 · what not to key on

Three keys that look right and are not

Arrival time

A retry arrives later; a reversal arrives earlier. Time is exactly what varies.

Amount and merchant

Two genuine coffees five minutes apart look identical. One would be swallowed.

Our own ID

The network does not know it, so its retry cannot carry it.

The only key both sides share is the network's reference.
Interviewer · the second-level push
“The partition fails over mid-write. Or the message table is purged, and a retry arrives a day later.”
Pause and answer before you continue
Challenge 3 of 8 · the second fix

One commit, and a long enough memory

Failover

The transaction commits on a quorum of replicas before the answer leaves: a new primary has both writes, or neither.

Retention

Keep message records longer than the network's retry and reversal windows: days. 20 M a day × 100 B × 30 days ≈ 60 GB.

The backstop

Past the window, clearing is the truth: a hold that should not exist expires; a clearing that should exist posts.

If asked, I'd say
“Every message is keyed by the network's reference, and its record is written in the same atomic step as the hold. A retry gets the stored answer; a reversal that arrives first waits as a tombstone for its original.”

Challenge 4 of 8 · the fix

A slice of the budget, and a table for when it runs out

MechanismRules always run, in process. The model gets 60 ms; then the decision proceeds without it, by a fail-policy table with an hourly exposure cap per cell.
PropertyL1: the answer leaves inside 300 ms whatever the model does. The quality loss is bounded by the cap, priced, and owned.

Challenge 4 of 8 · sequence

The cut-off, then the table

Challenge 4 of 8 · why not hedge

Sending the model call twice is not the answer

Hedged requests

Send the call to two scorers and take the first answer. It trims a random tail.

Why not tonight

Tonight the slowness is the model itself. Hedging doubles its load exactly when it is struggling, and every copy is slow.

A cut-off bounds our latency whatever the cause. Hedging only helps when the slowness is random.
Interviewer · the second-level push
“It lasts all evening and the cap runs out. And who decided those numbers?”
Pause and answer before you continue
Challenge 4 of 8 · the second fix

Tighten by cell; own it before the incident

Risk

Sets the limits in each cell from loss data; reviews them monthly.

Finance

Signs the cap: the most we accept losing while degraded.

Engineering

Makes each cell a setting, counts every degraded decision, pages at half the cap, rolls the model back by version.

When a cell's cap is used: step-up, then lower limits, then decline. The conversation happens before the incident, not during it.
If asked, I'd say
“The model gets a fixed slice of the budget, and the decision never waits past it. When it's late, a table owned by risk and finance decides, under an hourly exposure cap. We answer in time, and we know what that costs.”

Challenge 5 of 8 · the counter, as code

Count this swipe, then read ten minutes

def swipes_10m(store, token, now_s):
    minute = now_s // 60
    key = f"v:{token}:{minute}"
    pipe = store.pipeline(transaction=True)
    pipe.incr(key)                       # this swipe
    pipe.expire(key, 11 * 60)            # buckets clean up
    for m in range(minute - 9, minute):
        pipe.get(f"v:{token}:{m}")       # the nine before
    res = pipe.execute()
    return res[0] + sum(int(x or 0) for x in res[2:])
Two detailsThe swipe is counted before the decision, so declined attempts count: a script that keeps trying is the strongest signal. And every value goes into the decision record, so training sees what the model saw.

Challenge 6 of 8 · the honest limit

Shadow cannot say: labels arrive in 30 to 90 days

What shadow shows

How many it would decline today, and its overlap with existing rules. Not which were fraud: chargebacks arrive weeks later, and most declined good customers never complain.

What to use instead

Backtests on labelled history. Fast proxies: “it was me” replies to a decline text; step-up pass rates. A small slice with an alarm. And the expiry, so the rule is reviewed with real labels.

If asked, I'd say
“Rules are versioned data, not code: backtest, shadow, a small slice with a decline-rate alarm, then enforce, with a kill switch and the version on every decision. Shadow can't measure false positives, because labels arrive months later.”

Challenge 7 of 8 · the second fix

Idempotent files, and expiry that waits

Idempotent ingestion

A checksum per file; a unique ID per record. 3 % × 10 M = 300,000 duplicates become no-ops.

Expiry waits

Holds past their lifetime are released only after the day's file is in, so a late file finds its holds still open.

Reconcile daily

Posted in the auth store must equal the ledger, per account. Any difference is a break with an owner. The 09:00 target slips with a note, not silently.

If asked, I'd say
“Stand-in decisions are facts, not requests: we record every advice once, even when it overdraws the account, and model over-limit as a state. Clearing files are ingested idempotently, and hold expiry waits for the day's file.”

Recap · common mistakes in this round

Ten ways this round goes wrong

Design

  • Debit at authorisation
  • One authorisation, one clearing record
  • Read, then write, on the balance
  • Ignoring retries and early reversals
  • Velocity only from a stream

Judgment

  • “The network gives us 100 ms”
  • Waiting for the model, or “fail open” as the whole answer
  • Shadow mode to measure false positives
  • Refusing advices that overdraw
  • No owner for the fail policy
The close
Answer in time with a hold, not a debit. Make every message count once, whatever the order. Spend the budget on what matters and write down what happens when something is late. And record what the network decided for us.
