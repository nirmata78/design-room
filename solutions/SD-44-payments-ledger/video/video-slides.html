Requirements · functional

Five things the system must do

FR1 · Pay

Authorise and capture a payment, automatic or manual capture. Idempotent per client key.

FR2 · Adjust

Void, partial or full refund, chargeback. Each references the original payment.

FR3 · Notify

Every state change reaches the merchant, as a signed webhook, and internal consumers.

FR4 · Reconcile

Match our books against each processor’s settlement file daily. Every difference becomes an owned break.

Any account’s balance at any time, from the journal.

Theory
What makes this problem hard, in words that outlive any product name

Theory · 1 of 6 · what it is
One customer intent becomes one movement of money, recorded as balanced, immutable entries, and kept in agreement with the parties that actually move it.
Theory · 1 of 6 · what it is not

Four things it is confused with

The payment system is not the ledger

One orchestrates an intent across outside parties. The other is the book of record. They fail differently.

A ledger is not a balance table

A balance is a sum over entries. A number that is updated in place cannot explain itself.

Idempotency is not message de-duplication

It is one effect per intent, with the original answer returned to every retry.

Exactly-once delivery is not exactly-once effect

Across a network we can have the second. Never the first.

Theory · 2 of 6 · the two properties

State safety and liveness separately

Safety · must never happen

S1 One intent moves money twice.
S2 A journal transaction that does not balance, per currency, in integer minor units.
S3 History rewritten: a correction must be a new transaction.

Liveness · must eventually happen

L1 Every payment leaves “unknown” for a final state: in minutes by inquiry, at the latest at settlement.
L2 Every difference with the outside world becomes an owned break within a day.
L3 Every state change reaches the merchant.

Theory · 3 of 6 · the central tension

Speed of the answer against certainty about the truth

WhenWhat we knowMechanism that lives there
2 secondsapproved, declined, or nothingidempotency key · the unknown state · answer pending
minutesthe processor's answer to our questionthe inquiry job, by our own reference
next daythe processor's booksreconciliation against the settlement file
monthsa disputea new, adjusting transaction, dated now
No mechanism replaces another. Each one closes what the earlier ones left open.
Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 1 of 7

An idempotency key, inserted first

Property it protects

S1: one intent, one effect.

How it works

The client sends a key. We insert it, unique per client, with a hash of the request, in the same transaction that creates the payment. The final response is stored on the same row.

Failure it rules out

A retry or a concurrent duplicate creating a second payment; a changed retry silently accepted.

What it costs

A table with a retention window; an “in progress” answer the client must handle.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 2 of 7

An explicit state machine with “unknown”

Property it protects

S1 and L1.

How it works

Every payment moves through named states. A timeout or an ambiguous answer moves it to UNKNOWN, a real state with its own exits. The attempt is committed before the call goes out.

Failure it rules out

A timeout treated as failure, which double-charges on retry; or as success, which loses money.

What it costs

More states and transitions to test. Clients see “pending”.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 3 of 7

An immutable double-entry journal

Property it protects

S2, S3, and the audit.

How it works

Every movement is a transaction of entries whose debits equal its credits, per currency. Entries are inserted, never updated. A balance is a projection.

Failure it rules out

Unexplainable balances, lost cents, a hot row lock on a balance.

What it costs

Storage that grows forever. Reads need projections.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 4 of 7

A transactional outbox

Property it protects

L3.

How it works

The event is a row written in the same transaction as the state change. A relay reads committed rows and publishes them, at least once.

Failure it rules out

A state committed with no event, or an event for a state that rolled back.

What it costs

A relay to run. Events at least once, so consumers de-duplicate by event ID.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 5 of 7

Inquiry and reversal by our own reference

Property it protects

L1, and S1 on retry.

How it works

Each attempt carries a reference we generate. An inquiry asks the processor for that reference's status; an unwanted approval is reversed by the same reference.

Failure it rules out

An unknown that stays unknown; a retry the processor cannot recognise as the same attempt.

What it costs

A job per processor, rate-limited. Processors differ in what they support.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 6 of 7

Reconciliation with expectations and owned breaks

Property it protects

L2, and S2 against the outside world.

How it works

The ledger produces what it expects each processor to settle. The raw settlement file is matched line by line. Every unmatched line is a break with a type, an owner and an age.

Failure it rules out

Silent divergence from the counterparty's books.

What it costs

A pipeline, a finance-operations workflow, and people.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 7 of 7

Sub-accounts or one writer for a hot account

Property it protects

Throughput where a check needs a current balance.

How it works

Split a hot account into N sub-accounts that receive writes in turn and are summed; or send the rare balance-checked operation through one writer per account.

Failure it rules out

A merchant serialising on one row; a payout larger than the funds.

What it costs

Summing N rows; routing writes; a second place to be wrong.

D, and what it gives up

What D buys

Never guessing. A retry is harmless, an unknown has an exit, every balance is a sum, and the outside world is checked daily.

What D gives up

V3 · the pay path

Commit, call, commit again

def pay(req, key):
    row = idem.insert_or_get(req.client, key, hash(req))   # unique; same txn as below
    if row.existing: return row.response or conflict("in progress")
    p = db.commit(new_payment(req, state="AUTH_PENDING", attempt=1))  # evidence first
    try:
        r = adapter.authorise(p, ref=f"{p.id}/1", deadline=1.9)
    except Timeout:
        db.commit(p.to("UNKNOWN"))                      # the inquiry job takes over
        return pending(p)
    return db.commit(p.apply(r), entries(r), outbox(r), idem_done(key))
No branch guesses. A crash anywhere leaves a row that says how far we got.

Challenge 1 of 8 · the timeout

What does a timeout mean?

Naive answer“Mark it failed and let the client retry.” Or: “Retry it ourselves until it works.”

Challenge 1 of 8 · the second fix, and the honest limit

Fail over only what definitely failed

A breaker per processor

Stop sending new attempts to a processor that is down. New payments go to a second processor, if the contract allows.

Never fail over an unknown

An unknown sent again elsewhere is two possible charges. Unknowns wait, age, and show on a dashboard; the merchant sees pending.

Honest limit: a processor with neither inquiry nor idempotent references can only be resolved by the file, a day late. That is a reason to choose processors.
If asked, I'd say
“A timeout is an unknown, not a failure. I commit the attempt before calling out, move the payment to unknown, answer pending, and an inquiry job asks the processor by our own reference until it knows. Retries carry the same reference, so they can't become a second charge, and the settlement file is the final arbiter.”

Challenge 2 of 8 · the code

Insert or read, in twelve lines

def insert_or_get(client, key, req_hash, create):
    with db.transaction():
        try:
            db.insert("idempotency", client, key, req_hash, status="processing")
            return New(create())            # the payment, same transaction
        except UniqueViolation:
            row = db.get("idempotency", client, key)
    if row.req_hash != req_hash: return Error(422, "key reused")
    if row.status == "processing": return Error(409, "in progress")
    return Stored(row.response)
Why it worksThe result is returned, not re-executed. A changed request under an old key is a client bug, so it is reported, not hidden.

Challenge 2 of 8 · the second fix

A stuck “processing” row is just an unknown

The rows say how far it got

Key: processing, with the payment ID. Payment: AUTH_PENDING, with the attempt reference. That is exactly an unknown outcome.

Sweep, then inquire

A processing row older than a minute goes to recovery: challenge one's inquiry, by reference.

Retention: keys live seven days. The contract says a retry after that is a new payment.
If asked, I'd say
“The idempotency key is inserted first, with a unique constraint, in the same transaction that creates the payment. One request wins; the others get ‘in progress’ or the stored answer. A different body under the same key is a client bug, so I reject it instead of hiding it. And a crash in the middle is just an unknown outcome, which I already handle.”

Challenge 3 of 8 · the second fix

Lag is a metric; the database is the backstop

The relay

Outbox age is a metric with an alert. The relay is partitioned by account, so one slow partition does not hold the rest.

The merchant

Webhooks retry with backoff for days. After that, the merchant is told to reconcile with GET. Their dashboard reads our database, not the stream.

If asked, I'd say
“I never write to two systems and hope. The event is a row in the same transaction as the state change, and a relay publishes it at least once. Consumers de-duplicate by event ID, and the merchant can always ask us for the state directly.”

Challenge 4 of 8 · the fix

Append entries; the balance is a projection

MechanismPayments only insert their own entries: no shared row. A projection updates balances seconds behind. Where a check needs a current balance: 16 sub-accounts, summed, or one writer per merchant for that rare operation.
PropertyThroughput from removing the shared row from the hot path; correctness of the rare check from serialising only that check.

Interviewer · the second-level push

Pause and answer before you continue
Challenge 4 of 8 · the second fix, and the honest limit

Serialise only what must be serial

Per payment: a conditional update

One payment is never hot. refunded + amount ≤ captured in one statement on its row.

Per merchant: one payout writer

Reads the sum of the sub-accounts, then posts the payout transaction. Payouts are rare next to payments.

Honest limit: the projection lag must be measured and stated to the payouts team.
If asked, I'd say
“Five thousand updates a second to one balance row would serialise on its lock. So payments only append entries and the balance is derived. Where a check needs the balance now, like a payout, I split the account into sub-accounts or serialise just that rare operation through one writer.”

One more transaction, and a rate that is recorded

Dispute won

“A distributed transaction with the bank.” A balance column as the truth. A timeout treated as a failure. Floats. Keys checked instead of inserted. A changed retry silently accepted.

Events published outside the transaction. History edited. No reconciliation, or reconciliation by totals. No owner for breaks. Dashboards with no money on them.
