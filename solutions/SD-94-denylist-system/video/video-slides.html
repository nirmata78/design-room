Clarifying question 1 of 6
Who owns the list, and who calls the check?
A policy team, security and legal, owns the content. The platform team owns the mechanism. The check runs on every serving node: about 100,000 nodes in 20 regions.
Clarifying question 2 of 6
What is banned?
Addresses first: single IPv4 and IPv6 addresses and ranges. A second key type, a user id, will come. Do not design it out.
Clarifying question 3 of 6
How big, and how fast does it change?
10 million entries today, perhaps 100 million later. About 5 changes a second, 50 in a burst. 1 million requests a second at peak across the fleet.
Clarifying question 4 of 6
How fast must a ban take effect?
Within 30 seconds for 99.9 % of nodes. Removals follow the same rule.
Clarifying question 5 of 6
What if a node cannot get updates?
That is yours to propose. I will decide who signs it.
Clarifying question 6 of 6
Where does the list come from?
Mostly an internal tool. One list is mirrored from an external authority that publishes a daily bulk file. A variant has only a slow lookup API.
Requirements · functional

Five things the system must do

FR1 · Publish

Add or remove a key or range, with a reason, a source and an optional expiry. Validated. Audited.

FR2 · Distribute

Every change reaches every node within the bound. A node that was offline catches up on its own.

FR3 · Enforce

A verdict for each request, inside the request path, in microseconds.

FR4 · Inspect and explain

Which version each node runs, and how old. Why was this key denied or served on that day.

FR5 · Roll back and stop

Halt a rollout, kill a list, revert. An emergency fast path. Audited.

Requirements · behaviour

Three promises that are not numbers

Keep enforcing

A node holds its last known list through any outage of the distribution plane. Stale is not open.

The bound is signed

Each list has a maximum age and an action when it is exceeded, chosen by the owner.

A bad push is caught by the pipeline

Not by customers, and not by whoever happens to be on call.

Requirements · polling, stated once

A poll asks for the manifest. It does not download the list.

Every 10 s

A node asks the manifest: about 100 bytes with the current version, the delta window and a checksum.

If behind, inside the window

It fetches deltas: the changes between its version and now.

Snapshot only to bootstrap

Or after falling outside the window, or when a check fails. Never on a routine poll.

“Pull every ten seconds” never means “download the file every ten seconds”.
Requirements · scope

In scope, out of scope

addresses and ranges·allowlist·regional layers·audit

In scope

  • Single addresses and ranges, IPv4 and IPv6
  • A second key type as an extension
  • An allowlist of ranges never blocked
  • Internal and mirrored sources
  • Staged rollout, audit, operator console

Out of scope, on purpose

  • Detecting abuse: a model or rules engine (SD-43)
  • Rate limiting (SD-25)
  • Absorbing a volumetric attack: scrubbing upstream
  • Content rules inside requests: a web firewall
  • An appeals product: an owner and a workflow
Requirements · say it aloud

An address is not a person

Shared and carrier-grade addresses

One address can hold thousands of legitimate users. A single wrong range blocks all of them.

Why it shapes the design

An allowlist that always wins. Removals as carefully handled as adds. A softer action than a hard block where it fits.

The core
The core is four things: a versioned list, a distribution path that can be verified, a local check, and a release discipline.

What makes this problem hard, in words that outlive any product name

Theory · 1 of 6 · what it is
A versioned set of banned keys, copied to every node, checked without a network call, changed under a release discipline.
Theory · 1 of 6 · what it is not

Four things it is confused with

Not a rate limiter

No counters, no window. The verdict depends on the key alone.

Not a cache

A cache may be wrong by design and refills on a miss. A denylist must hold the whole set.

Not a feature-flag service

Same push mechanics. But a wrong flag changes behaviour, and a wrong block drops a customer.

Not a firewall

It decides on identity, not on content.

Theory · 2 of 6 · the two properties

State safety and liveness separately

Safety · must never happen

S1 A banned key is served by a node whose list is newer than that ban plus the bound.
S2 An allowlisted or legitimate key is denied because a bad, partial or mis-merged list was applied.

Liveness · must eventually happen

L1 Every node applies every published version.
L2 99.9 % of nodes do so within 30 s.
L3 A node that cannot update is detected, reported and handled by the stale policy.

Why two safety propertiesA denylist can be wrong in both directions. Most designs defend S1 and forget S2.
Theory · 3 of 6 · the central tension

Freshness against blast radius

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 1 of 7

An in-process check on a compact structure

Property it protects

Latency and independence.

How it works

A sorted array or hash set for single keys, a prefix trie for ranges, held in the server's memory. The request never leaves the process.

Failure it rules out

A network call on every request, and the list service becoming part of every request's availability.

What it costs

The whole set in RAM on every node. Staleness becomes a design parameter instead of a non-issue.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 2 of 7

Versioned snapshots and deltas, with checksums

Property it protects

Convergence and integrity: S1, S2, L1.

How it works

One writer assigns a strictly increasing version. Each version has a snapshot and a delta from the previous one. Each checksum covers its content and the checksum before it.

Failure it rules out

A node unable to say what it holds, partial or corrupt state, out-of-order application, a full download per change.

What it costs

A compile step, a delta log to retain, and two artifact kinds to store.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 3 of 7

A relay tree, push plus a pull backstop

Property it protects

Propagation speed and resilience: L1, L2.

How it works

Origin, regional relays, zone relays, nodes. A push says a version exists. A ten-second manifest poll is the backstop. Artifacts are immutable and cacheable.

Failure it rules out

Origin overload, and a missed push leaving a node behind forever.

What it costs

More hops add latency. Two paths to build, operate and test.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 4 of 7

Atomic swap and a last known good copy on disk

Property it protects

Safety of the node's serving state: S2.

How it works

Build the new structure beside the old, verify, swap one pointer. Write the verified snapshot and version to disk.

Failure it rules out

A half-applied list, an empty list after a restart, a crash during an update.

What it costs

Two copies in memory briefly. A startup path that must work with no network at all.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 5 of 7

Release discipline: gates, a kill flag, rollback

Property it protects

Safety against bad content: S2.

How it works

Limits and an allowlist at ingest. Stages gated automatically on block rate. A flag in the manifest nodes honour within one poll. A rollback publishes the old content as a higher version.

Failure it rules out

One mistaken rule reaching the whole fleet at once.

What it costs

Minutes of delay for a routine push. An emergency fast path with fewer gates. A gate that must be trusted.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 6 of 7

A staleness bound with an explicit action

Property it protects

Liveness made visible: L3.

How it works

Each list has a maximum age and an action when exceeded, signed by its owner: alert, or drain the node, with a cap.

Failure it rules out

Silent drift: every node quietly serving an old list for days.

What it costs

A policy someone must sign. Capacity lost when nodes drain.

Theory · 4 of 6 · mechanism 7 of 7

A filter in front, an exact check behind; allow beats deny

Property it protects

Correct verdicts at scale: S2.

How it works

A compact filter answers “definitely not listed”. A maybe is confirmed by an exact tier. An allowlist range overrides any deny.

Failure it rules out

A false positive blocking a real user. A wrong range overriding a protected one.

What it costs

A second tier for confirmation. Precedence rules to explain and to test.

V2 · how the three arrows between a relay and a node work

Poll, push, delta

Poll · every 10 s

The node asks the manifest with its version. About 100 bytes. The backstop that always works.

Push · optional

The relay says “version N exists”. The node then does the same manifest step at once. It carries no data.

Delta · when behind

Small, immutable, verified, applied. A snapshot only to bootstrap or beyond the window.

Push shortens the wait. Poll guarantees arrival. The node's logic is the same for both.
V2 · the objection

One bad version reaches everyone in eighteen seconds

Nothing checks the content

A typo is as fast as a ban. The pipeline cannot tell them apart.

Nobody decided what stale means

A node that cannot update does something, and nobody chose it.

The distribution is correct. The release discipline is missing. That is version three.
High-level design · version 3 of 3

V3Safe enforcement: a controller, gates, and a stated policy

atomic swap·last known good·staged rollout·staleness bound

V3 · what the node does

One loop, with a safe failure at every step

// every ~10 s, with jitter
m = manifest(have = version)                     // ~100 bytes
if m.killed: enterKillMode()                   // honoured in one poll
if m.current == version: return              // the common case: nothing
art = (version >= m.minDelta) ? delta(version) : snapshot(m.current)
if !verifyChain(art, m.checksum): discard(); report(apply_failed); return
new = buildBeside(current, art)                  // old list still serving
swap(new); persist(new); report(applied, age = 0)
Every branch ends serving a good list. A failure never leaves the node worse off.

What it has not been tested against

A node two hours behind. Changes out of order. A plausible but wrong rule. No control plane. A list that does not fit. A slow authority. A silent failure.

Challenge 1 of 7 · the node that was offline

What should the node fetch, and is it whole?

Naive answer“On reconnect it downloads the whole list again, and we check each node's file.”
Why it failsNothing tells the node what it needs, and nothing proves what arrived is complete.
Challenge 1 of 7 · the trace

A node two hours behind, in numbers

02:00node z7-0412 applies version 8,000,000. Then it loses its uplink
04:00the list is at 8,360,000: 360,000 changes missed ≈ 11 MB of deltas

Label the numbersIllustrative. The shape matters: a small difference, a large naive transfer, and no integrity check.
Challenge 1 of 7 · the fix

A manifest, a delta window, a snapshot fallback

MechanismThe node sends the version it has. The manifest says: take the delta, or take the snapshot. Every artifact is verified against a chained checksum before anything is applied.
PropertyConvergence (L1) and integrity (S2): a node can always reach the present, and never serves a state it has not verified.
Challenge 1 of 7 · sequence

Reconnect, manifest, delta, verify, swap

Challenge 1 of 7 · the decision, as code

Three branches, each safe

m = manifest(have = v)
if m.current == v:            return                 // up to date
if v >= m.minDelta:            art = delta(v)         // recent: small
else:                          art = snapshot(m.current)  // too old
if !verifyChain(art):         discard(); art = snapshot(m.current)
if !verifyChain(art):         keepLastGood(); report(apply_failed)
Why it worksA node can always reach the current version: by a small delta if it is recent, by a snapshot if it is not. It verifies before it applies. The fleet answer to “is everyone on N” is the reported version, not a hope.
Interviewer · the second-level push
“The delta window is twenty-four hours and the node was down for three days. Or the checksum fails again on the snapshot.”
Pause and answer before you continue
Challenge 1 of 7 · the second fix, and the honest limit

Beyond the window, take a snapshot. If that fails, stop and say so

Beyond the window

The manifest points to the snapshot, whatever the node's age. The zone relay caches it: fifty nodes cost one origin fetch.

A checksum that keeps failing

A corrupt artifact at the origin. The node keeps its last known good, reports apply_failed, and the control plane halts that version.

A node that can reach nothing keeps an old list. That is challenge four.
If asked, I'd say
“Every version has a number and a checksum chained to the one before. A node says what it has, and the manifest says whether a small delta or a full snapshot gets it to the present. It verifies before it applies and swaps atomically, so it never serves a half-built list. And I know the fleet is converged because every node reports the version it runs.”

Interviewer · the second-level push

Pause and answer before you continue
Challenge 2 of 7 · the second fix, and the honest limit

Bounded tombstones, and a counter that never rewinds

Tombstones

Kept only for the delta window. A node older than the window takes a snapshot, which has none, so nothing is resurrected.

The version counter

Lives in the rule store's transaction log. A restore that would rewind it is refused. After a rebuild, start with a higher epoch: never reuse a number.

If asked, I'd say
“I do not trust arrival order. One writer assigns versions, and each key remembers the version of its last change, so a late or duplicate message is ignored. Nodes protect themselves; the kill flag is on its own channel

A local circuit breaker

If a node's block rate jumps by a multiple of its baseline for N seconds right after an update, it reverts to the previous version on its own, reports, and pages. No central decision needed.

A separate, tiny channel

The kill flag is one field in the manifest every poll reads. Not a new snapshot. It works even if the artifact path is the broken one.

A precise, plausible, wrong rule cannot be found by statistics. Only the allowlist, review of high-impact changes, audit and a fast removal path find it.
If asked, I'd say
“The same pipe that delivers a ban in ten seconds delivers a mistake in ten seconds, so a push gets the same discipline as a binary: validation, an allowlist that always wins, a canary gated on block rate, a kill flag on its own channel, and a rollback that is just a new version. Nodes also protect themselves: if the block rate jumps right after an update, they revert on their own.”

A drain budget, and a risk someone accepts in writing

A drain budget

Automatic draining never removes more than a fixed share of the fleet: say 5 % per region. Past that, nodes keep serving and the page goes to a human.

Cache the verdict

A positive verdict is cached on the node with a bounded lifetime. Repeated hits from one source cost one lookup.

“A shared lookup per request puts the list service in everyone's availability, and a millisecond on every call. Recap · common mistakes in this round

Ten ways this round goes wrong

Design

  • A lookup per request on the path
  • “Eventually consistent” with no number
  • No versions or checksums
  • Trusting arrival order
  • A full download per node per change

Judgment

  • Failing closed fleet-wide, or open silently
  • Never asking who owns the failure policy
  • A Bloom filter used as a verdict
  • One big push, no canary, no allowlist
  • A green relay dashboard as proof
The close
The check is local because it must be fast and independent. The list cannot be, so I gave it versions, a bound on how old it may be, and the discipline of a release. So a ban lands quickly and a mistake does not.
