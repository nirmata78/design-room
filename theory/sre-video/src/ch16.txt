## 16.0 Why look outside software

= cards c3 | Other industries | Veterans and their fields
grn Telecommunications :: Emergency calling systems. Users expect the phone to work, and the consequence of failure can be **fatal**.
grn Medical devices and surgery :: Laser eye surgery: strict **regulation**, careful improvement, gradual acceptance.
grn Lifeguarding and defence :: Training, drills, and attention to detail, because **lives** or vehicles are at stake.
> The point: high-stakes fields converged on the same practices. Software is catching up.
N: Engineers who came to software from other fields tell the same story. Telecommunications, including emergency calling, where users expect the phone to work and failure can be fatal. Medical devices and eye surgery, with strict regulation and careful improvement. Lifeguarding and defence, where training, drills and attention to detail matter because lives or vehicles are at stake. The point is that high-stakes fields converged on the same practices, and software is catching up.

## 16.1 A summary of best practices

= table sm | Appendix B · Best practices | A selection, with the reason
Practice | In one line
**Fail sanely** | Validate configuration. On a bad input, keep running on the previous state and alert
**Progressive rollouts** | Stage changes by capacity and by geography. If something is unexpected, **roll back first**, diagnose after
**Define objectives like a user** | Measure at the user's point of view, for example at the client
> The appendix has more: monitoring, postmortems, capacity, overload, and the shape of SRE teams.
N: Here is a compact summary of best practices, and three entries repay a close reading. Fail sanely: validate configuration, and on a bad input, keep running on the previous state and raise an alert. Progressive rollouts: stage changes by capacity and by geography, and if anything unexpected happens, roll back first and diagnose after. And define objectives like a user: measure at the point of view of the user, for example at the client. There is more, on monitoring, postmortems, capacity, overload, and the shape of S-R-E teams.

= cards c2 | Appendix B · Two examples | Why validation is a reliability feature
red An empty file :: A global traffic system accepted an empty configuration file, caused by a file permissions problem. For about **six minutes** it answered that every name did not exist.
red A single character :: A list of suspect addresses was replaced by a single forward slash. It matched **every** address, and the whole web was marked as dangerous.
> Both were valid files with **implausible** content. Check meaning, size and change, not only syntax.
N: Two examples from the appendix. A global traffic system accepted an empty configuration file, caused by a file permissions problem, and for about six minutes it answered that every name did not exist. In another, a list of suspect addresses was replaced by a single forward slash, which matched every address, and the whole web was marked as dangerous. Both files were valid, and both were implausible. Check the meaning, the size and the size of the change, not only the syntax. And remember the empty list in episode three: it is the same failure.

= th | Appendix B · the line to say | If a new input looks implausible, keep the old one, and tell a human.
V: When configuration changes look implausible, keep running on the previous version, and tell a human. A system that accepts any input will one day accept the worst one.

= dia | New material · AI serving | The same ideas applied to a model-serving system
e16-ai-serving
N: Machine-learning serving did not exist at this scale in 2016, and these ideas apply directly. Take a request to a service that runs a large model. It enters through a gateway, where admission control can limit work in units of tokens or compute, not requests, since one request can be a hundred times the cost of another, which is the lesson of queries per second from episode ten. It waits in a queue, which must be bounded, with deadlines.
N: It runs on accelerators, which are scarce and slow to scale. It may use a cache of earlier results. And the model, the prompt and the data around it are all changes that can fail, so they go through a staged rollout, with evaluation as the health gate.

= table sm | New material · AI serving | The ideas, translated
Idea | In a model-serving system
Indicators (ep 2) | Time to first output, throughput, **cost per request**, and a quality score from evaluation, not just availability
Overload (ep 10) | Admission control by tokens or compute; a smaller model or a cached answer as the **degraded mode**
Releases (ep 5) | Staged rollouts of model and prompt versions, with automated evaluation as the gate and an instant revert
Capacity (ep 14) | Accelerator capacity is the scarce resource: forecast it, reserve it, and plan for loss of a zone
Data (ep 13) | Freshness of retrieval data, provenance, and a way to delete on request
Incidents (ep 7) | Treat a model or prompt regression as a **change-induced** emergency, with a rollback path
> Mappings, not claims about any one product.
N: Here are the translations. Indicators: time to first output, throughput, cost per request, and a quality score from evaluation, not only availability. Overload: admission control by tokens or compute, with a smaller model or a cached answer as the degraded mode. ## 16.4 Putting it together

= probe | You have three minutes. Describe the operations layer of a new payments service.
- **Objective**: availability and latency measured at the client, with an **error budget**
- **Monitoring**: golden signals, with symptom-based pages and a burn-rate alert
- **Releases**: staged, with automatic rollback; **idempotency keys** make retries safe
- **Failure**: overload controls, a budgeted retry, graceful degradation, a restore drill
- **People**: on-call rota, runbooks, and a postmortem practice
C: I start with the objective: availability and latency measured at the client, with an error budget that governs the pace of change. Monitoring on the golden signals, with symptom-based pages and a burn rate alert. Releases are staged, with automatic rollback, and idempotency keys make retries safe. For failure, I plan overload controls, a budgeted retry policy, graceful degradation, and a restore drill. And for people, an on-call rota, runbooks and a postmortem practice.

= probe | The error budget is spent and the chief executive wants to ship. What do you do?
- Apply the **agreed policy**, which was written before
- Offer **ways to ship safely**: smaller scope, staged rollout, kill switch
- Put the **risk in numbers**, and let the business owner **decide in writing**
- Afterwards, review whether the objective was right
C: I apply the policy that was agreed in advance, because the middle of an argument is the wrong time to write one. I offer ways to ship more safely: a smaller scope, a staged rollout, a kill switch. I put the risk in numbers, and ask the person who owns the business outcome to decide, in writing. And afterwards we review whether the objective was set at the right level.

= probe | What would you cut with half the time and half the budget?
- Keep what protects **users and data**: objectives, rollback, backups with a **tested restore**
- Cut **breadth**: fewer dashboards, fewer custom tools, fewer features
- **Defer**: multi-region, fancy automation, until the objective needs it
- Write down what you **chose not to do**, and the trigger to revisit
C: I keep what protects users and data: a clear objective, a fast rollback, and backups with a tested restore. I cut breadth: fewer dashboards, fewer custom tools, fewer features. I defer multi-region and clever automation until the objective needs them. And I write down what I chose not to do, with the trigger that would make me revisit it. A justified omission is a design decision, not a gap.
