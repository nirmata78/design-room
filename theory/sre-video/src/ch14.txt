## 14.0 The story that starts it

= dia | The opening story | A service meets Santa
e14-santa
N: Start on Christmas Eve, two thousand eleven. A satellite imagery service normally serves up to several thousand images a second. That night it received twenty-five times its normal peak, upward of a million requests a second. The cause: Santa was coming. Google had worked with a national air defence organisation on a website that tracked Santa's progress around the world, with a virtual fly-over built on satellite imagery.
N: Here is why this was a hard launch: a hard deadline, because nobody could ask Santa to come a week later, huge publicity, an audience of millions, and a very steep ramp-up, since everyone would be watching at once. Everything about it was predictable except how many.

## 14.1 A role and a checklist

= list | The launch coordination role | What the dedicated team does

- **Curates** the launch checklist and the recipes for common problems
- **Drives** the launch: keeps momentum, removes blockers
- **Signs off** launches judged safe, and educates the developers
- Gives developers the **documentation** to integrate with shared services
N: Google's answer to launch risk was a specialist role. A small team of launch coordination engineers audit services before they launch, and curate a checklist of questions and recipes for common problems. They drive the launch, by keeping momentum and removing blockers. They sign off launches judged safe, and they educate the developers, with documentation for integrating with shared services. The role is part consultant, part gatekeeper, and part teacher.

## 14.2 Capacity for the day that matters

= table sm | One level down | Headroom is arithmetic, not optimism
Zones | Capacity needed in total, to survive losing one zone at peak | Spare capacity
2 | 200% of peak | 100%
3 | **150%** of peak | 50%
4 | 133% of peak | 33%
5 | 125% of peak | 25%
> Formula: with Z equal zones, losing one leaves Z minus 1, so each must carry peak ÷ (Z minus 1), and the total is Z ÷ (Z minus 1) times peak.
N: One level down: headroom is arithmetic, not optimism. Suppose you must survive losing a zone at peak. With two zones, each must be able to carry the whole peak, so you need two hundred percent of peak in total. With three zones, the remaining two must carry it, so each carries half the peak, and the total is a hundred and fifty percent. With four zones, a hundred and thirty-three percent. With five, a hundred and twenty-five.
N: The formula is Z over Z minus one, times the peak. More zones make resilience cheaper, up to a point, which is one reason large systems spread widely.

= table sm | Capacity · The launch version | What to add on top of the steady-state forecast
Factor | Why | Example treatment
Launch spike | Interest peaks on day one | Plan for a multiple of normal peak, from the product forecast
Dependencies | A shared service may not have your headroom | **Tell its owners** the forecast and the date
Warm-up | Caches and connection pools start cold | **Pre-warm**, and ramp gradually
Quota and limits | You can hit a cap you did not know about | Check the platform limits **before** the date
> The Santa story: the imagery service met 25 times its normal peak.
N: And the launch version of capacity planning. Add the launch spike, because interest peaks on the first day, and plan from the product forecast. Account for dependencies: a shared service may not have your headroom, so tell its owners the forecast and the date. Warm up caches and connection pools, which start cold, and ramp gradually. And check quotas and limits before the date, because you can hit a cap you never knew about. The Santa story shows what a service meets when launch interest arrives: twenty-five times its normal peak.

## 14.3 Techniques for a gradual launch

= th | The line to say | Headroom is arithmetic.
V: Headroom is arithmetic. With Z zones, and one lost, you need Z over Z minus one times the peak, not a feeling.
[pause: 0.8]

= table sm | Selected techniques | Four techniques
Technique | What it does | Watch for
**Gradual, staged rollout** | Exposes the change to a few percent, then more, by region or by user | Interacts with caches, and with users who see two versions
**Feature-flag framework** | Turns code on per user or per percentage, without a deploy | **Flag debt**; untested combinations
**Dealing with abusive clients** | Detects, limits and isolates clients that misbehave | Legitimate heavy users caught by the limit
**Overload behaviour and load tests** | Finds the breaking point and shows what happens beyond it | Test in a safe environment, and keep within committed limits
> Very few launches at Google are push-button, all-at-once.
N: There are four techniques. Gradual, staged rollouts expose the change to a few percent, then more, by region or by user, and you watch for interactions with caches and with users who see two versions. Feature flag frameworks turn code on per user or percentage without a deploy, and the risks are flag debt and untested combinations. Dealing with abusive clients means detecting, limiting and isolating those that misbehave. And overload tests find the breaking point and show what happens beyond it. Very few launches at Google are of the push-button kind.

= panel trap | The trap | Gatekeeping every launch the same way
Treating a copy change and a global product launch alike either **wastes** the reviewers' time or **under-protects** the big one.
**Tier** launches by risk: small, internal, reversible ones take a light path. Large or irreversible ones get the full review.
Keep **driving convergence and simplification** of the checklist, so it stays used.
N: The trap is gatekeeping every launch the same way. Treating a copy change and a global product launch alike either wastes reviewers' time or under-protects the big one. Tier launches by risk: small, internal, reversible changes take a light path, and large or irreversible ones get a full review. Keep driving convergence and simplification, so the checklist stays short enough to be used. A checklist nobody reads has no effect.

= panel now | Since 2016 | The checklist became code
**Production readiness** is increasingly checked by automation: does the service have an objective, an alert, a rollback, a runbook, a dashboard?
**Feature flags and experiment platforms** are standard, with kill switches for every risky new path.
**Waiting rooms and admission control** let a service survive a demand spike you cannot capacity-plan for, by queueing or limiting entry.
**Game days** rehearse the launch, including the failure of a dependency, before the real date.

## 14.5 Probe drills

= probe | Product wants to launch to everyone on day one. What do you say?
- Ask what the **hard constraint** is: a date, a press event, an exclusivity
- Offer a **staged** path that meets it: internal, then a small share, then wider, **within the same day** if needed
- Prepare a **kill switch** and a waiting room as the safety net
- Quantify the risk in **error budget** and users
C: First I find out what the hard constraint really is: a date, a press event, an exclusivity. Then I offer a staged path that still meets it, which can be internal, then a small share, then wider, even within the same day. I make sure there is a kill switch and a waiting room as the safety net. And I put the risk in numbers: how many users, how much error budget, so the decision is the business's, made with eyes open.

= probe | You have a hard launch date and an unknown demand. How do you plan capacity?
- Take a **range** from product: low, expected, and high
- Provision for **expected plus headroom**, with the ability to **scale quickly**
- **Load test** to the high case, and learn the breaking point
- Plan **graceful degradation** and admission control for beyond the high case
C: I get a range from the product team: a low, an expected, and a high case. I provision for the expected case plus headroom, with a way to scale fast. I load test up to the high case, so I know the breaking point. And for demand beyond the high case I plan graceful degradation and admission control, so that the failure is a slower or smaller service, not a dead one.

= probe | What are the first three questions in a launch review?
- What **depends** on this, and what does it depend on?
- What happens when **it fails**: each dependency, each region?
- How do we **roll back**, and how do we **know** it is going wrong?
C: First, what depends on this, and what does it depend on, because hidden dependencies cause most surprises. Second, what happens when it fails: each dependency, each region. And third, how do we roll back, and how do we know it is going wrong, which means the signals and the stop conditions. Those three cover architecture, failure modes and rollout in one pass.
