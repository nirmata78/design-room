## 12.1 Cron, run at scale

= table | Idempotency | Whether you can launch twice, and whether you can skip
Job | Safe to launch twice? | Safe to skip one?
Garbage collection every five minutes | **Yes** | Yes: the next run catches up
Monthly payroll | Maybe not | **No**
Newsletter to a million people | **No** | Sometimes
> The default: prefer **skipping** to double-launching, because a skip is easier to recover from.
N: First, a table. Garbage collection every five minutes is safe to launch twice, and safe to skip, because the next run catches up. Monthly payroll may not be safe to repeat, and must not be skipped. A newsletter must not be sent twice, and skipping it is sometimes acceptable. The default, as far as the infrastructure allows, is to prefer skipping to double launching, because recovering from a skip is more tenable than recovering from a double. And owners should monitor the effect of their jobs, so that a skip is noticed.

= dia | The mechanism | The two synchronisation points of a launch
e12-cron-launch
N: How does a distributed cron service decide? Follow the picture. The service has one leader and several followers, and they keep their state in a consensus-backed log, as in the last episode. For every launch there are two synchronisation points. Before the launch, the leader records in the log that it is about to launch. Then it sends the launch request to the datacenter scheduler. After the launch, it records that the launch finished. Now imagine the leader dies between the two records.
N: The new leader reads the log and finds a launch that started and never finished. It does not know whether the request was sent.

= table | One level down | What the new leader does with an unfinished launch
Condition | Action
External operations are **idempotent** | Repeat them. Safe
The state of the external system can be **looked up** | Check whether the job was launched, then act
Neither | Choose per job: **skip** or **launch again**
> Look up the state if you can; make operations idempotent if you can; otherwise decide **in advance**, per job.
N: One level down. There are conditions. If every external operation is idempotent, the new leader repeats them, and that is safe. If the state of the external system can be looked up, the new leader checks whether the job was launched, and acts accordingly. If neither is true, the new leader must choose, per job, to skip or to launch again, and that choice is made in advance, by the job's owner. This is the general shape of every exactly-once-looking system: it is at-least-once delivery, with idempotency or a lookup to make the repeat harmless.

= th | The line to say | Prefer skipping to double launching.
V: Unless the owner says otherwise, prefer skipping a launch to launching twice, because a skip can be repaired and a double often cannot.
[pause: 0.8]

= panel key | Scaling | What changes at a large scale
**Thundering herd**: thousands of jobs set for midnight hit the scheduler and its dependencies together. Let owners use jittered or hashed times.
**State is small, but must be consistent**: the log holds launch records, and followers hold snapshots to recover quickly.
**Failure of the scheduler is not failure of the jobs**: running jobs go on while the leader changes.
N: Now what changes at scale. Thundering herds: thousands of jobs set for midnight hit the scheduler and its dependencies together, so let owners use jittered or hashed times instead of round ones. The state is small, but it must be consistent: the log holds launch records, and followers keep snapshots so that recovery is quick. And a failure of the scheduler is not a failure of the jobs. Jobs that are running keep running while the leader changes.

= th | The line to say | At-least-once plus idempotency looks like exactly-once. Without idempotency, choose skip or double in advance.
V: A scheduler cannot know whether it launched. So it records its intent before the side effect and its completion after, and the job's owner decides in advance whether an unknown launch is skipped or repeated.
[pause: 0.8]

## 12.2 Pipelines

= dia | The load shape | Three periodic pipelines that overlap
e12-moire
N: Start with the simplest pipeline: a program that runs periodically, say every hour or every night, reads input, and writes output. It works at small scale. It breaks at large scale, and the failures have names. Uneven work distribution: one slow shard holds up the whole run. Monitoring problems: a pipeline that is silent looks the same as one that is idle. The thundering herd: everything starts at the top of the hour.
N: And the moiré load pattern, which is in the picture. Three pipelines, each periodic, each fine on its own. Occasionally their peaks line up, and the shared resource sees the sum. The peaks cause on-call pain, and no individual pipeline owner can see them.

= th | The line to say | Alert on freshness, not on exit codes.
V: A periodic pipeline hides its failures. Alert on freshness and backlog, not on whether the last run exited cleanly.
[pause: 0.8]

= table | One level down | Why periodic pipelines suffer at scale
Problem | Cause | A direction
Uneven distribution | A few large shards run long, while most workers sit idle | Smaller work units, dynamic assignment
Thundering herd | Everything scheduled at the same moments | Continuous processing, or staggered starts
Moiré load | Independent schedules sometimes align | Shared capacity planning; jitter
Weak monitoring | Only a finished or failed run is visible | Report progress, **freshness** and backlog
> The remedy is a continuous pipeline that processes work as it arrives.
N: One level down. Uneven distribution comes from a few large shards that run long while the other workers sit idle, so use smaller work units and assign them dynamically. The thundering herd comes from everything being scheduled at the same moments, so use continuous processing, or stagger the starts. Moiré load comes from independent schedules that sometimes align, so plan the shared capacity together, and add jitter. Weak monitoring comes from seeing only a finished or failed run, so report progress, freshness and backlog. The remedy is a continuous pipeline that processes work as it arrives.

= dia | The mechanism | Leases and uniquely named output
e12-workflow
N: Now how a continuous pipeline stays correct. A coordinator, called the task master at Google, holds the state of every unit of work, as pointers to uniquely named data. Workers are stateless. A worker acquires a unit of work with a lease, and may only commit it while it holds a valid lease. Now imagine a worker that stalls, loses its lease, and wakes later. It is an orphan.
N: To keep it from doing damage, every output file a worker opens has a unique name. The orphan can keep writing, but it writes to its own file, and when it tries to commit, it cannot, because another worker holds the lease. This is the double correctness guarantee: unique outputs, and state controlled by leases.

= th | The line to say | An orphan can write. It cannot commit.
V: Give every output a unique name, and every commit a lease. An orphaned worker can write, but it cannot commit.
[pause: 0.8]

= table | The alternative and the price | Batch, continuous, and durable workflow
Style | Strength | Weakness
Periodic batch | Simple, cheap to reason about | Herds, stragglers, stale results, silent failures
Continuous pipeline | Fresh results, even load | A coordinator to run, and idempotency to design
Durable workflow engine | Resumes after a crash by **replaying an event history** | Your code must be **deterministic**
> The middle row is the classic form. The last row is the modern form of the same idea.
N: The alternatives. Periodic batch is simple and cheap to reason about, with herds, stragglers, stale results and silent failures. A continuous pipeline gives fresh results and an even load, and needs a coordinator, and idempotency by design. A durable workflow engine resumes after a crash by replaying an event history, at the cost that your code must be deterministic. The middle row is the classic form, and the last row is the modern form of the same idea.

= panel trap | The trap | A pipeline that stopped, and nobody noticed
Alert on **freshness**: the age of the newest output, against what the consumer needs.
Alert on **backlog growth**, not only on errors.
A pipeline with a **clean exit and no output** is a failure. Treat empty results as suspicious.
N: The trap is a pipeline that stopped, and nobody noticed. Alert on freshness: the age of the newest output, against what the consumer needs. Alert on backlog growth, not only on errors. And treat a clean exit with no output as a failure. Empty results are suspicious, because it is the quietest way to fail.

## 12.3 Then and now

= panel now | Since 2016 | The same ideas, in current tools
**Scheduled jobs on a container platform** have settings for what to do on overlap and on a missed start. They encode the skip or double choice. Read the defaults.
**Workflow orchestrators** model pipelines as dependency graphs, with retries, backfills and alerts on lateness.
**Durable execution engines** keep workflow state as an event history, so code resumes after a crash.
**Streaming engines** give continuous processing with event time and checkpoints. The design-room course covers them in the logs and streams part.
N: What has changed? Scheduled jobs on container platforms have settings for what to do when runs overlap, and when a start is missed. They encode the skip or double choice, so read the defaults. Workflow orchestrators model pipelines as graphs of dependencies, with retries, backfills and alerts on lateness. Durable execution engines keep workflow state as an event history, so that code resumes after a crash. And streaming engines give continuous processing with event time and checkpoints, which the design-room course covers in its logs and streams part.

= th | Then and now · the line to say | Data quality is an indicator.
V: Treat data quality as a service level indicator: freshness, completeness and correctness, each with a target.
[pause: 0.8]

= table | Then and now | The data-quality angle
Dimension | What to measure | Why
**Freshness** | Age of newest output versus the need | Stale data is wrong data, quietly
**Completeness** | Share of expected input that arrived | A partial run looks like a run
**Correctness** | Checks against invariants or a second source | Wrong output is worse than no output
**Lineage** | Where each output came from | Debugging, and deletion obligations
> These become SLIs for pipelines, as episode two described.
N: One addition. Pipelines now have data quality objectives. Freshness: the age of the newest output against the need. Completeness: the share of expected input that arrived, because a partial run looks like a run. Correctness: checks against invariants or a second source, because wrong output is worse than none. And lineage: where each output came from, for debugging and for deletion obligations. These become the service level indicators of a pipeline, as episode two described.

## 12.4 Probe drills

= probe | The scheduler dies after starting a job and before recording it. What happens?
- The new leader sees **no record** of the launch, so it launches again: possible **double**
- Fix: **record intent before** the side effect, and completion after
- On recovery, look up the job's state, or rely on **idempotency**
- Otherwise the job's owner chose skip or repeat in advance
C: Without a record, the new leader cannot know, so it may launch again, which gives a double launch. The fix is to write the intent to the replicated log before the side effect, and the completion after. On recovery, an unfinished launch is resolved by looking up the job's state, or by relying on idempotency. And if neither is possible, the job's owner has already chosen whether to skip or repeat.

= probe | A million jobs are scheduled for midnight. What breaks, and what do you do?
- The scheduler, the cluster, and shared dependencies see a **spike**
- Spread the load: **jitter or hashed** start times, and a start window
- **Rate-limit** launches, with a queue and priorities
- Make the jobs **tolerate** delay, and monitor lateness
C: What breaks is everything that sees the spike: the scheduler, the cluster scheduler, and the shared services the jobs call. I spread the load with jitter or hashed start times inside a window, rate-limit launches with a queue and priorities, and make the jobs tolerate a delay. And I monitor lateness, not just failure, so I can see the herd forming.

= probe | How do you make a pipeline step safe to run twice?
- Give each unit of work a **unique, deterministic ID**
- Write output to a **unique name**, then **commit atomically**
- Use **conditional writes** or a deduplication table keyed by that ID
- Commit state only while holding a **valid lease**
C: I give each unit of work a unique and deterministic identifier. Output goes to a uniquely named location, and is committed atomically, so a repeat cannot corrupt a previous result. Writes to shared state are conditional, or checked against a deduplication table keyed by that identifier. And a worker can only commit while it holds a valid lease, so an orphan cannot commit.
